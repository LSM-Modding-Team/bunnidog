# Combat companions — adventures worth designing for

These are **proposed player experiences**, not descriptions of implemented
combat. They test the recommendations in [the combat plan](COMBAT_COMPANION_PLAN.md).
The animals below are examples of useful combinations, not classes, required
recipes or names the player must learn.

The short player-facing promise should fit on a few lines: bond with an adult,
choose it as a fighting partner, fight together, whistle to call it back, and
care for it if hurt. Different families will be good at different adventures.
Everything below must support that promise without making the player study it.

## 1. “Can my ordinary first bunnidog actually help?”

I have an ordinary loyal adult, a sword and a Whistle. I have not discovered
rare genes or built a laboratory. I deliberately enable Assist and attack a
Zombie. My partner approaches from beside me, bites and resets; it does not
stand between my crosshair and the enemy. Its help buys me time to block or
place a torch. Whistling calls it back even while the Zombie is still alive.

This should be a good first encounter with a zero-point adult. If a routine
fight with a participating player repeatedly downs it, baseline survival,
attack timing and positioning need work. “Breed a stronger one first” or “craft
armor before using it” would make the introduction fail.

## 2. “Watch my back while I mine.”

I bring a skittish, large-eared partner into a cave. While I face the wall it
notices a visible approaching threat, turns towards it and gives a distinct,
brief alarm. A Creeper makes it create space rather than rush in. I turn,
move away and deal with the Creeper myself. The animal contributed something
I care about even though its damage was low.

All companions should react sensibly to an obvious fuse; a lookout is better
at early attention and keeping an exit, not the only animal with basic danger
recognition. Better ears/Senses extend useful awareness, without detecting
every hidden chamber. One meaningful warning beats constant barking and chat.
If lookouts spend entire encounters running without helping, their short safe
skirmishes and warnings need improvement before adding more damage penalties.

## 3. “Hold this doorway while I shoot.”

A stocky, slate-coated stoic waits at a safe doorway with Assist enabled. It
contests a Zombie coming through while I shoot from behind. It stays near the
held position instead of chasing another target down a corridor. When hurt,
it yields the opening and falls back; a later braced body check could make
that interception more distinctive.

This is a sturdy companion, not a magical tank. Armor does not stop every
damage type, a Skeleton can still shoot me, and holding a position does not
rewrite every enemy's target. Friendly arrows must pass my own partner, and
it must leave room for me to retreat through the door. Otherwise the supposed
defender is my largest obstacle.

## 4. “Take that archer; I'll handle this Zombie.”

I choose a feral, fleet-legged lean animal for fast, directed strikes. I Focus
a reachable Skeleton while dealing with something else. It commits to that
enemy instead of watching me as attentively as a loyal partner would. Its bite
is stronger, but its light body cannot absorb a long exchange. I recall it
before it overcommits; a heard command works without an obedience roll.

A springy sibling would offer a different later opportunity: a short visible
pounce onto a verified landing. It cannot also inherit fleet as its expressed
leg type. A closed door or lava gap defeats either approach; the animal should
tell me it cannot reach safely. Focus must be quick enough to use while moving.
If a contextual panel is too slow, improve that gesture rather than accepting
menu wrestling as the price of a feral line.

## 5. “Help me build without starting a war.”

A loyal partner follows me while I repair an outdoor wall. A nearby Zombie
actually attacks me; the animal protects me. It does not sweep the countryside,
provoke a neutral Enderman, kill a villager or hunt my livestock because it
happens to be hungry. My other carriers remain carriers rather than joining
as an accidental army.

I can turn Assist off when I want everyone to retreat. A territorial feral
guard at my Nest remains a separate, deliberately assigned home role. Taking
one animal adventuring does not unhouse it, turn every resident into a fighter
or make a distant home guard obey expedition Focus orders.

## 6. “Which family would I take on this trip?”

For a Nether supply run, an ember-coated partner can keep fighting through
fire that would force another to withdraw. It still needs a safe route and
does not make a lava lake a walkable shortcut. A moss cave partner is useful
against poison; bringing that same animal against Wither Skeletons exposes a
different danger. I have an actual breeding/packing choice, not one generic
resistance rating.

An End expedition offers another choice: a void coat avoids Levitation, while
Phase legs can escape after a nonlethal hit. Neither saves a pet falling into
the void. Phase should not immediately run back into the same bad fight. A
regional advantage is memorable because of a situation it changes, and useful
even before adding a special attack for every coat.

## 7. “I'm not leaving my favourite breeding line at home.”

My named partner loses an ordinary fight and collapses. It is out of the
encounter, not an invulnerable shield. I finish the fight before helping it;
there is no hidden bleed-out timer demanding I open a menu while being hit.
Residual combat burning/Wither should not invalidate that promise. Standing in
lava or another continuing terrain hazard is a different, visible emergency.

On safe ground I spend a real plain guava on first aid. If I need extraction,
an empty Travel Basket can take the downed animal with its name, genome, cargo,
bond and injury. At a quiet camp it eats real food and recuperates while I do
something else. It cannot rejoin by being captured and released. A Nest/Trough
is convenient at home, not a mandatory hospital before it can recover.

The consequence is losing a fighter and changing my plans, with a rescue story
worth remembering. If ordinary adventures mostly become repeated five-minute
waiting periods, change survival/recovery tuning rather than calling that depth.

## 8. “Can we fight together without fighting our pets?”

My friend uses a bow; I use a sword. We bring different partners and choose
nearby targets. Ordinary weapon crossfire should not punish either player's
bonded animals, even on a casual co-op server where we have not configured
scoreboard teams. A downed animal does not absorb enemy arrows for us either.
We can provide care/rescue without acquiring each other's keeper bonds or
issuing each other's combat commands.

Three active fighters per keeper is a starting hypothesis. Two keepers can
therefore bring six; a common enemy may fall quickly, and a cramped room may
need fewer partners. Test that actual co-op situation. Do not assume that a
single-player party limit proves multiplayer balance or good pathing. Kill
rewards still happen once, and Looting on my sword is not copied to every bite.

## 9. “Come back means come back.”

I realize the cave fight is going badly. One normal Whistle use calls the
party out; it neither releases my bond nor attacks the thing under my crosshair.
A feral striker disengages too. Taking another hit does not reset its retreat.
It regroups instead of immediately remembering the same enemy and charging
again. I can leave, heal, or deliberately order another attack.

If I die or disconnect, my partners stop offensive pursuit and wait safely
near their last position. On returning I can consult a last-seen breadcrumb,
not a live scanner. They do not teleport to my bed, load distant chunks, run
cross-country to an old Nest or farm kills for an absent keeper. Once genuinely
safe after an encounter, I can choose a different healthy partner; this is not
a permanent roster lock because something exists behind a wall.

## 10. “Now I have a reason to raise another litter.”

After those trips I know what I want: a tougher door defender, a more attentive
lookout, a fire-safe striker or a faster-recovering travelling family. Calipers
describe what a real animal does; existing seed/family tools help me choose
parents and follow actual offspring. I choose improvements for my adventures,
not whichever animal has the largest universal combat score.

Two loyal parents carrying feral can produce feral pups: before mutations,
their temperament outcome is 75% loyal and 25% feral. The loyal parents do not
occasionally become disobedient because they carry that hidden copy. The new
feral adult's different habits are the breeding result. Its keeper bond is
learned individually, never inherited. I can try it in a modest encounter or
later optional practice area without grinding kills to unlock its own genes.

Developed lines should eventually outgrow ordinary Zombies. That is a reward
for breeding, not a reason to secretly scale every local enemy up. Difficult
terrain, mixed threats, higher-risk encounters and choosing the right partner
can keep decisions interesting without erasing progression.

## What these scenarios should settle before expanding the ability list

| Question from playing | Design response to try first |
| --- | --- |
| Is an ordinary partner more burden than help? | Fix baseline survival, contact timing and recall; do not gate usefulness behind rare genes, points or armor. |
| Does every breeder choose maximum feral damage? | Make keeper protection, attention, recovery and positioning matter; modest damage differences cannot carry all temperament identity. |
| Does no one want a skittish partner? | Give its warning and safe skirmishing real opportunities; do not disguise an idle non-fighter as a support role. |
| Is directing a target too awkward during a fight? | Shorten the Focus interaction while preserving an unconditional normal-use recall. |
| Does a three-animal party block movement or just erase encounters? | Adjust spacing, engagement and party size from actual mixed/co-op fights; respect native hurt windows. |
| Does defeat create a good rescue or constant chores? | Protect the ordinary recovery window and tune frequency/aftercare; no invisible death clock or repeated feeding clicks. |
| Does a developed line trivialize its old first-night fight? | Allow earned progression; assess it on harder adventures rather than nullifying the breeding result. |

These are encounter/playtest goals for later implementation, not a request to
write dozens of mirrored automated tests during a documentation-only batch.
